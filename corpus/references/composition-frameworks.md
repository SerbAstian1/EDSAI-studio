# Composition Frameworks — Serb's Visual Reasoning Vocabulary

## Role

This is not a department — it's a **shared visual-reasoning layer every department reads and applies**. It's the codified set of compositional structures Serb actually thinks in when placing anything inside a frame: a hero section, a poster, a logo mark, a packaging face, a social tile, a mind-map diagram. Any department making a layout or arrangement decision — Department 2 (world design/key art), 3 (poster... see 14), 5 (UI layout), 12 (mark construction), 13 (physical panel layout), 14 (poster) — names the structure(s) in play from this vocabulary rather than describing a layout only in adjectives ("balanced," "dynamic," "clean").

## The core failure this file exists to prevent

**Arranging instead of composing.** An arrangement is elements placed where they fit. A composition is elements placed according to a named structural logic that directs the eye in a deliberate order. The test, same as Department 14's: could you name, in one phrase, *which* structure is doing the work here — and does that structure actually serve the message, or is it just the first layout that looked fine?

Naming a structure is not decoration for the write-up. It's the mechanism that makes "why does this layout work" answerable instead of asserted.

---

## How to use this file

For any layout/composition decision:
1. **Name the dominant structure** from the catalog below (one primary; a secondary is allowed if genuinely load-bearing, e.g. rule-of-thirds placement *within* a radial composition).
2. **State why it fits the content** — what does this structure do for *this* message that a different one wouldn't (guide to a focal point, create tension, imply motion, hold calm symmetry)?
3. **State the eye-path it produces**, in one sentence — same discipline Department 14 already requires for posters, now the default for any framed composition.
4. **Check it against Failure Conditions** below before calling the composition decided.

A structure named without a stated reason is the same failure as a score without justification elsewhere in EDSAI: don't do it.

## The Catalog

Organized by what the structure *does* to the eye, not alphabetically — pick the family that matches the intended effect first, then the specific structure within it.

### Family: Grid & Proportion (calm, structured, editorial)
- **Rule of Thirds** — frame divided into a 3×3 grid; place the subject/focal point on a gridline or intersection rather than dead-center. Default choice for natural-feeling balance without being static.
- **Golden Section / Golden Ratio** — divisions based on the ~1.618 ratio rather than even thirds; slightly more refined/organic proportion than a plain thirds grid. Reach for this when the brief calls for "premium" or "classical" over "modern/grid-y."
- **Golden Spiral / Fibonacci Spiral** — the golden ratio expressed as a spiral, leading the eye inward toward a focal point along a curved path rather than straight gridlines. Strong for imagery with implied depth or motion (staircases, natural forms, any subject with its own spiral logic).
- **Spiral Section** — a simpler nested-rectangle version of the golden proportion; use where the golden spiral's curve is too ornate for the deliverable (e.g. a UI grid vs. a poster).

### Family: Radial & Focal (draws attention to one point)
- **Radiating / Radial** — lines or elements emanating from a single point; pulls the eye immediately to that point and implies energy, explosion, or importance. Strong for a hero product shot, a launch announcement, a single dominant CTA.
- **Focal Mass** — a cluster of small elements (dots, icons, a crowd) against a comparatively empty field; the density itself becomes the focal point, distinct from radial because there's no single origin point, just concentration vs. sparseness.
- **Circular** — a strong circular or orbital shape frames or contains the subject; reads as complete, contained, protective, or cyclical. Common for badges, seals, product-hero isolation.

### Family: Line-Driven (directs the eye along a path)
- **Converging / Leading Lines** — real or implied lines (a road, architecture, shadows) that converge toward a point, pulling the eye there. The strongest tool for "make sure the eye lands *here*."
- **Diagonal / Double Diagonal** — one or two diagonal axes across the frame; inherently more dynamic and tense than horizontal/vertical because diagonals read as instability-in-motion, not rest. Use when the brand energy is kinetic, urgent, or youthful — avoid when the brand needs to read as stable/trustworthy.
- **Vertical Lines** — repeated verticals (columns, trees, high-rises); read as aspiration, growth, or formality; can also feel like a barrier/cage depending on density and gap.
- **Horizontal Lines** — repeated horizontals (horizon, shelving, stacked bands); read as calm, stability, rest — the default "trustworthy" line direction.
- **Cross** — one strong horizontal and one strong vertical intersecting; a very quiet, almost architectural balance point — good for restraint-heavy, minimal-brand compositions where a single intersection is the whole story.
- **Tunnel** — receding frame-within-frame lines (an archway, a corridor) that pull the eye toward a distant point; combines leading lines with actual depth compression — strong for "reveal" or "destination" messaging.

### Family: Weighted Shapes (implies stability, tension, or motion through geometry)
- **V-Shape / V-Arrangement** — elements forming a V, converging toward a base point; reads as funneling attention downward/inward, or (inverted) as expansion/opening upward.
- **Pyramid / Triangle Composition** — a stable, wide-based triangular arrangement; the most classically "stable and grounded" weighted shape — use for anything needing gravitas, permanence, trust.
- **Golden Triangles / Harmonious Triangles** — diagonal-plus-perpendicular triangle divisions of the frame (not the same as a simple pyramid); used to divide asymmetric compositions while keeping proportional harmony — a more dynamic cousin of the golden section for diagonal-heavy imagery.
- **L-Arrangement** — elements forming an L, anchoring two adjoining edges of the frame and leaving the opposite corner open as negative space; a strong, quiet asymmetric-balance move.
- **C-Shape** — an arc or C-curve of elements around a partially enclosed negative-space center; frames a focal point without fully surrounding it the way Circular does — feels more organic/gestural than the closed Circular structure.
- **Compound Curve / S-Curve** — an S-shaped path through the frame; the classic "graceful, meandering, editorial" path — strong for fashion, lifestyle, anything wanting elegance-in-motion over the Diagonal family's raw energy.

### Family: Balance Logic (how weight is distributed, independent of shape)
- **Symmetry** — both halves mirror; reads as order, formality, perfection, sometimes coldness if overused. Strongest where the brand truth is precision/reliability.
- **Asymmetry / Balance (unequal massing)** — unequal elements balanced by weight rather than mirrored form (a large shape on one side offset by a smaller-but-denser or higher-contrast element on the other); reads as more human, more alive than symmetry, but requires the weights to be genuinely felt as equal — this is the difference between "balance" (intentional, felt-equal) and "unbalanced" (a genuine failure state, one side visually heavier with no compensating device on the other).
- **Juxtaposition** — two elements placed close together specifically to invite comparison or contrast (old/new, small/large, rough/refined); the composition's whole point is the relationship between the two things, not either one alone.
- **Contrast** — light/dark, large/small, rough/smooth, or busy/empty pushed deliberately far apart within the same frame to create drama and hierarchy; distinct from Juxtaposition in that contrast is about visual difference driving hierarchy, not necessarily inviting a comparison of meaning.

### Family: Depth & Frame (uses the space itself, not just shapes in it)
- **Negative Space** — deliberate empty area that isolates and emphasizes the subject; absence functioning as an active compositional element, not leftover space (see Department 14's identical requirement).
- **Framing / Frame Within Frame** — using an element within the scene (a doorway, an arch, foliage) to create a frame around the subject, emphasizing it and adding depth.
- **Fill the Frame** — the opposite move: subject/texture fills the entire frame edge-to-edge with no breathing room, for maximum intimacy/intensity or a texture/pattern-forward brand moment.
- **Depth (foreground/midground/background layering)** — deliberate layering so the eye reads three distinct distance planes rather than one flat plane; makes a static 2D surface feel dimensional.
- **Patterns & Repetition** — a repeated unit (texture, grid of icons, architectural repetition) establishes rhythm; a single deliberate break in the pattern becomes the focal point *because* everything else repeats.
- **Figure to Ground** — a single clear silhouette/subject stands unambiguously apart from its background at a glance — the most basic test any composition should pass regardless of which other structure is also in play (see Department 14's Figure-Ground Clarity requirement).

## Cross-Reference to Existing Departments

This file doesn't replace department-specific composition guidance already written — it's the shared vocabulary underneath it:

- **Department 2 (Creative Direction)** — when defining the aesthetic universe/key-art direction, name which 1–2 families above define the brand's default compositional character (e.g. "this brand lives in the Line-Driven/Horizontal + Grid family — calm, editorial, never Radial or Diagonal").
- **Department 5 (UI Design System)** — grid structure (5.2) should state whether it's a plain column grid, a Golden Section-informed grid, or a Rule-of-Thirds-anchored hero layout; hero/landing compositions should name their structure explicitly rather than just stating column counts.
- **Department 12 (Brand Ideation)** — construction logic (12.4) for an abstract/geometric mark should name which structure the mark's construction is built on (Circular containment, Golden Spiral proportion, Cross-based letterform grid, etc.) — this makes "construction feasibility" concrete instead of a vague geometric gesture.
- **Department 13 (Print/Packaging)** — panel-by-panel layout (13.2) and any single-face layout (business card, label) should name its structure per face, same discipline as a poster.
- **Department 14 (Poster & Composition)** — 14.2's "grid or structural logic" question is answered directly from this catalog; the cinematic reference-standard moves (teaser/withhold, ghost-type, spotlight) are themselves usually built on Negative Space + Figure-to-Ground + one Weighted Shape or Radial structure — name all that apply.

## Failure Conditions
- A layout described only in adjectives ("clean," "balanced," "dynamic") with no named structure from this catalog behind it
- A named structure that doesn't match the stated eye-path (e.g. calling something "Radial" when the actual visual weight sits in a corner, not at a center point)
- Defaulting to Rule of Thirds or plain Symmetry on every single deliverable regardless of brand energy — those are the two most common defaults precisely because they're safe; safe-by-default is a failure mode like any other unexamined habit in this system
- "Balance" claimed for a composition that is actually just **Unbalanced** — one heavy element with nothing on the other side doing compensating visual work
