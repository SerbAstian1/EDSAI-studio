# Client Simulation Mode

## Purpose

Real briefs come from a real-world business context with real constraints — budget awareness, deadline pressure, an actual stakeholder who will react to the work. This reference governs how EDSAI calibrates tone, risk tolerance, and anticipated objections based on the implicit client archetype in a brief. State the archetype assumption explicitly if it isn't stated by the user — never silently assume.

## Client Archetypes

### Startup Client
- **Posture**: speed-focused, conversion-focused, lean systems
- **Risk tolerance**: higher — willing to ship something opinionated and iterate, less precious about getting every detail perfect on V1
- **What this changes**: favor faster build paths, simpler component systems, conversion-critical sections get the most design attention, polish sections further from the conversion path can be lighter

### Corporate Client
- **Posture**: structure-heavy, conservative design, high trust emphasis
- **Risk tolerance**: lower — unconventional choices need stronger justification, consistency and predictability often outweigh novelty
- **What this changes**: favor proven UI patterns over experimental ones, heavier emphasis on credibility signals (proof, certifications, structure) in copy and UX, motion stays restrained and functional rather than expressive

### Luxury Brand Client
- **Posture**: restraint, minimalism, emotional depth, premium perception
- **Risk tolerance**: low on anything that reads as cheap or busy, but high tolerance for slow pacing and whitespace that a conversion-optimized brief would reject
- **What this changes**: typography and whitespace carry more of the weight than copy density; CTAs are quieter and fewer; motion is slow and deliberate (top of the timing ranges in Department 6); never use urgency-driven conversion tactics (countdown timers, "X people viewing this") — they read as a category mismatch against premium positioning

### Creative/Cultural Brand Client
- **Posture**: expressive design, experimental motion, narrative richness
- **Risk tolerance**: high — generic/safe is the actual risk here, not unconventional choices
- **What this changes**: Department 2's polarities can sit further toward Expressive/Artistic/Organic without that being a problem; motion budget (Department 6) can be spent more freely as long as it still passes the mute test; copy can take more narrative risk

## Anticipated Client Objections

Before presenting work as final, proactively run it against the objections a real client of the identified archetype would likely raise. This isn't paranoia — it's catching the same gap a real review would catch, before the real review happens.

Common objections to pressure-test against:
- *"This feels too complex"* — re-check Department 4's cognitive hierarchy; is there a decision point that could be cut?
- *"We need something simpler"* — distinguish whether this is a real simplicity gap or a request to remove brand distinctiveness; the right response addresses complexity, not distinctiveness, unless the client explicitly wants the latter
- *"Can this convert better?"* — re-check Department 3's Copy Flow Model for a skipped step, and Department 4 for a decision point with insufficient information scent
- *"This feels too experimental"* — check whether the client archetype actually supports the current Department 2 polarity positions, or whether the brief implied more conservative risk tolerance than was used
- *"Is this too expensive-looking for our audience?"* — check Department 5's color/type choices against the audience psychology from Department 1; premium signaling can mismatch budget-conscious audiences even when the brand wants quality perception

## Response Rule

When responding to real or anticipated feedback: **diagnose the actual problem, briefly explain the cause, then propose the improved version.** Do not defensively justify the original choice — even if the original reasoning was sound, a client raising a concern deserves a direct answer about what changes and why, not a re-explanation of why the original was fine. If the original genuinely was right and the objection is based on a misunderstanding, clarify briefly and move on — don't over-explain.
